Display settings¶

Quality¶

Quality sets how detailed the picture is: shadows, lights, water, the glow around strong lights, and how far the terrain and plants keep their detail. It has three levels.

Level What changes For whom
Low Blurrier shadows up close (3 maps of 1,024 pixels), 256 lights at once, simpler water (no refraction), smaller glow, detailed terrain up to 512 m, plants up to 60% of their distance, fewer particles at once (in the game). An integrated graphics chip (most laptops).
Medium Shadows almost as sharp as High (3 maps of 2,048 pixels instead of 4), 512 lights at once and fewer light shadows, water like High, a glow as wide but computed smaller, detailed terrain up to 768 m, plants up to 80% of their distance. The editor's views, by default; a player whose graphics card struggles.
High The finest everywhere: 4 shadow maps of 2,048 pixels, 1,024 lights, water with refraction and scenery reflections, detailed terrain up to 1,024 m, plants at their full distance. A graphics card: the game's picture as you want it.

Where to set it¶

  • For the editor's views: Preferences, Display section, View quality (Medium at first). It only applies to you, in the editor. A scene's Shadow quality (Sky and light) never goes above it there.
  • For the game: it chooses at startup, from the player's computer (Low on an integrated chip, High on a graphics card). The game started with Play does the same: it does not take the editor's view quality.
  • For the player: with the Menu system, Options, Graphics, Quality (see below).

The editor's views only work when the picture changes¶

Whatever the quality, the editor only redraws a view when its picture would change: the camera moves, an object changes, a view setting changes, or something moves on its own (visible water, particles, terrain that is still arriving). Otherwise the view keeps its previous picture. While you type a message to the agent, or while the agent works, the views cost almost nothing. With four views, the ones that are neither under the mouse nor being edited follow an object you move a little less often (20 times per second), and water 10 times per second; the view under the mouse follows everything at every frame (water, 30 times per second).

Upscaling¶

Upscaling draws the scene in an image smaller than the window, then enlarges it to the window's size. So as not to lose detail, it shifts the camera very slightly at every frame (by a fraction of a pixel) and blends the frames that follow one another: within a few frames, it knows more than a single frame shows. Object edges no longer look jagged ("staircase" edges), and the graphics card has fewer pixels to compute.

What enlarges the picture is the upscaling method:

  • None (default): WorldMint's own computation, on every graphics card;
  • FSR (all cards): AMD FSR 2, sharper, especially when the picture is much reduced; it works on every card and is already in WorldMint (see AMD FSR);
  • DLSS (NVIDIA): on NVIDIA GeForce RTX cards, if you provided it (see NVIDIA DLSS).

Where the chosen method doesn't work, WorldMint's replaces it, without telling the player.

Where to set it¶

  • For the game: nothing selected, Game window section, Upscaling (the level) and Upscaling method. The setting goes with the exported game. The agent can change it too (except choosing DLSS).
  • For the editor's views: Preferences, Display section, View upscaling and View upscaling method. It only applies to you, in the editor. A camera preview shows the game's setting.

The levels¶

Level The scene is drawn at What you get
Off (default) full size The image as before: sharp, but edges look jagged.
Anti-aliasing only (100%) full size Smooth edges, the best image. Costs a little more than Off.
Quality (67%) 67% of the width Almost as sharp, a little faster on a modest card.
Balanced (58%) 58% In between.
Performance (50%) 50% (a quarter of the pixels) Faster, a little softer when the camera moves.
Ultra performance (33%) 33% The fastest; blurry when the camera moves. For a very weak card or a very large screen.

What we measured, at 1920 x 1080, on the integrated graphics of an AMD processor: on a continent with its sea, 5.4 ms per frame without upscaling, 4.3 ms with Performance and 3.7 ms with Ultra performance; on a small village the gain is smaller (the scene costs little), and Quality is a little slower than Off there. On a powerful graphics card the difference doesn't show: the image is already very fast.

Good to know¶

  • When the camera stops, the image gets sharper for about a second: that is upscaling blending the frames. In the editor, the view keeps drawing during that time, then rests.
  • A screenshot taken by the agent is always taken at full size, without upscaling. A game capture (--capture) keeps its upscaling, with its method, and its anti-aliasing: the picture is redrawn there until its whole jitter is blended.

The player chooses too¶

With the Menu system, the game's Options button has a Graphics section: the player chooses the quality there (Low, Medium or High: see quality), the upscaling (the same levels as above), the anti-aliasing when upscaling is off (see anti-aliasing), the shadows (As quality at first, then High, Medium, Low or Off, for the sun and every light: see light and shadows) and the upscaling method (None, FSR, and DLSS if you provided it; the line only shows when FSR or DLSS works on their card). At first, they find what you set here (and the quality chosen for their machine); each click moves to the next choice, at once. Their choice is kept from one game to the next; your game settings do not change.

Anti-aliasing¶

When upscaling is off, anti-aliasing smooths the objects' edges, which otherwise look like stairs. (Upscaling, even "Anti-aliasing only", already does it: the choice then no longer matters.)

  • For the game: nothing selected, Game window section, Anti-aliasing. The agent can change it too, and the player chooses it in the game's menu (Display tab).
  • For the editor's views: Preferences, Display, View anti-aliasing (None at first). A camera preview shows the game's.
Choice What it does
Automatic (as quality) The game's starting setting: TAA in High and Medium quality, FXAA in Low (TAA would cost 4 ms there on an integrated chip). A project created before 11 November 2026 keeps SMAA in High and Medium: its picture does not change.
TAA (sharp in motion) Blends the frames that follow one another, like "Anti-aliasing only": the finest, but each picture is rebuilt from the previous ones.
SMAA (sharp, no trails) Recognizes the shape of each edge in the finished picture and smooths it: sharp, nothing trails behind what moves. In High, it finds edges by color; otherwise by brightness.
FXAA (lightest) A single pass over the finished picture: the cheapest, a little softer.
None The edges as they are.

What we measured, at 1920 x 1080, on a scene of 225 cubes (graphics card time on top of None):

RTX 5090 AMD Radeon integrated chip
FXAA + 0.01 ms + 0.85 ms
SMAA + 0.03 ms + 1.5 ms
TAA + 0.04 ms + 4.0 ms

Why TAA at first: on dense foliage, it shimmers a third less than SMAA (measured, see ADR-032). Its drawbacks: a slight trail behind what moves fast, a slightly softer picture. To get SMAA back, choose it in Anti-aliasing.

SMAA comes from its authors (Jorge Jimenez et al., MIT license); FXAA is written by WorldMint after the FXAA 3.11 algorithm.

AMD FSR¶

AMD FSR 2 (FidelityFX Super Resolution 2.2, MIT license) rebuilds the full-size picture from the reduced one, its depth and the motion of each point. It is sharper than WorldMint's method when the picture is much reduced (Performance, Ultra performance), and it works on every graphics card: NVIDIA, AMD or Intel. It is already in WorldMint: nothing to download or provide, and nothing to show in your game.

  • Choosing it: Upscaling method: FSR (all cards). Upscaling switches to Quality if it was off. With Anti-aliasing only (100%), it smooths edges without shrinking the picture.
  • Good to know: when the camera turns fast, in Performance, the grain of soft shadows may show for a moment (WorldMint's method smooths it more, by being blurrier).

What we measured, at 1920 x 1080, on a scene of grass, trees and banners (the graphics card's time for the view, on 11 November 2026):

RTX 5090 AMD Radeon integrated chip (2 cores)
Off (full size, no anti-aliasing) 0.64 ms 7.4 ms
WorldMint, Anti-aliasing only (100%) 0.40 ms 11.7 ms
WorldMint, Quality 0.26 ms 9.0 ms
FSR, Anti-aliasing only (100%) 0.53 ms 23.4 ms
FSR, Quality 0.36 ms 16.4 ms
FSR, Balanced 0.32 ms 14.9 ms
FSR, Performance 0.29 ms 14.2 ms

On the RTX 5090, FSR costs 0.1 ms more than WorldMint's method at the same level. (Off costs more than the others there: without upscaling, ray-traced shadows cast several rays per point.) On the small integrated chip of a desktop processor, it costs 5 to 12 ms more: WorldMint's method stays the fastest there; Performance saves 9 ms over Anti-aliasing only.

NVIDIA DLSS¶

On an NVIDIA GeForce RTX card, upscaling can go through NVIDIA DLSS, sharper than WorldMint's when the image is much smaller. On every other card, the game keeps WorldMint's, without telling the player anything.

WorldMint doesn't provide DLSS. It is NVIDIA software: you download it yourself from NVIDIA, and you are the one who distributes it with your game, under NVIDIA's license. For every game that uses it, even a free one, it asks:

  • NVIDIA's logo when the game starts and in its credits;
  • every use of this logo submitted to NVIDIA, which must approve it in writing, two weeks ahead;
  • to tell NVIDIA before a commercial release of the game.

Turning it on¶

  1. Download Streamline from NVIDIA (Streamline releases) and unzip it. Its files are in bin\x64.
  2. Nothing selected, Game window section: Upscaling method, DLSS (NVIDIA). A window says what the license asks; read it (Read NVIDIA's license), then check I have read it: I will take the steps with NVIDIA myself.
  3. Choose the folder: Streamline's bin\x64 folder. WorldMint checks that sl.interposer.dll, sl.common.dll, sl.dlss.dll and nvngx_dlss.dll are there and carry NVIDIA's digital signature. A missing, changed or otherwise-signed file is refused, with a message.
  4. Turn DLSS on. Upscaling switches to Quality if it was off: the chosen level applies to DLSS too.

For the editor's view: Preferences, Display, View upscaling method, DLSS (NVIDIA), from the next start of the editor.

In the exported game¶

The export copies NVIDIA's files into the game (nvidia folder, next to the program) only if DLSS is on, and reminds you of the logo and the steps. If they are missing, the game is exported anyway, with WorldMint's upscaling. The agent knows this setting but never turns it on: the agreement with NVIDIA is yours.

What we measured on an RTX 5090, at 1920 x 1080: about 0.4 ms more per frame than WorldMint's upscaling; the game takes about 3 seconds longer to start, while DLSS gets ready.

Ray tracing¶

Usually, a shadow is made by redrawing the scene as seen from the sun, several times per frame (the "shadow maps"): up close it is sharp, but it softens and loses detail further away. Ray tracing works differently: it casts real rays of light through a copy of the scene arranged to be searched quickly. Each point of the picture asks the sun whether it can see it; if an object is in the way, the point is in shadow. The shadow is accurate at every distance, and it can be soft like that of a real sun. The same rays make reflections: water, metal, glass and smooth floors reflect the scenery, even what is off screen.

Which cards have it¶

NVIDIA RTX cards (20 series and later), AMD Radeon RX 6000 and later, and Intel Arc. Some graphics chips built into the processor have it too, but they are too slow to use it in a game (see the measurements below). On every other card, nothing changes: the game is drawn with shadow maps, as before.

What each quality does¶

Quality With ray tracing
Low Never in Automatic: shadow maps, as before. The player can force it (On): it then takes Medium's settings, with a warning.
Medium The sun's shadow by a ray, up to 1 km around the camera. Placed lights keep their shadow maps. Ray-traced reflections on what is nearly smooth (roughness up to 0.3: water, polished metal, glass), up to 500 m.
High The sun's shadow up to 1.5 km, and the shadow of every placed light that casts one, by one ray each (with no limit on their number). Reflections up to a roughness of 0.5 (blurrier and blurrier), up to 1 km. Indirect lighting (which can be turned off on its own).

Shadow softness (in "Sky and light" for the sun, each light's own softness) applies to rays too: it is the size of what gives the light. With temporal anti-aliasing (the one of upscaling), one ray per point aims at a slightly different spot of the sun at every frame, and the frames are blended; without it, a few rays per point (4 in Medium, 8 in High). The rays' sun is never smaller than the real one (0.5°): a ray-traced shadow never has a perfectly sharp edge. At a softness of 0°, the edge also keeps the blur of the shadow maps: turning ray tracing on does not change the look of shadows, it makes them more accurate (at the foot of objects, far away). Shadows turned Off (the Shadows line of the player's menu, or the scene's shadow quality) turn the rays off too.

What keeps its shadow maps¶

Rays see a copy of the scene arranged in advance. What is missing from it keeps its shadow through shadow maps, next to the rays:

  • animated characters (their pose changes at every frame; a later step will put them in the copy);
  • objects whose creator shader cuts holes in their surface or moves their vertices (a ray would see the model whole and still).

Particles and water receive the rays' shadows, but do not cast any. A piece of terrain or a tile that has just arrived is arranged within a few frames: meanwhile, it casts no shadow (far away, it does not show).

Reflections¶

A ray leaves each point of water, metal, glass or a smooth floor, in the direction of the reflection. What it hits is lit as it would be on screen, more simply: its colour, the sun and its shadow, the sky. What reflects this way depends on the material's roughness (see materials): up to 0.3 in Medium, up to 0.5 in High. The rougher a surface, the blurrier its reflection; with temporal anti-aliasing, the ray aims in a slightly different direction at every frame, and the frames are blended. Beyond that roughness, or when the ray hits nothing, the reflection stays as before: the sky.

  • Realistic water keeps the screen's reflections first (they also show characters and all the light); the ray fills in what the screen cannot see, like the shore behind the camera.
  • Stylised water reflects the scenery in each facet, like so many small flat mirrors, slightly blurred and mixed with the sky: it keeps its flat colours.
  • Not reflected by the rays: animated characters, objects whose creator shader cuts holes in or moves their surface, particles, and the light of placed lights on what is reflected (at night, a reflection only shows what glows by itself).

Ambient occlusion (the soft shadow in corners) also uses rays, in Medium and High. Indirect lighting uses rays in High quality: the light bounced by surfaces (a sunlit floor lights the walls, a room is lit through its window), ray traced with a light cache. It costs about 1 to 2 ms per frame; on at first, it can be turned off on its own (see below). Off, or below High, shade gets the sky's ambient light, as before.

Automatic, On, Off¶

  • Automatic (at first): ray tracing only on a dedicated graphics card that has it, in Medium or High quality. Never on an integrated chip, never in Low.
  • On: always, on a card that has it, even where it is heavy (an integrated chip, Low quality). The player's menu then warns them: "Heavy on this card: the picture may slow down". There, only shadows use rays: reflections would cost too much (on an integrated chip, water went from 53 to 97 ms per frame).
  • Off: never; shadow maps, as before.

Where to set it¶

  • For the game: nothing selected, Game window section, Allow ray tracing (ticked by default). Unticked, the game is always drawn without it, on every machine, and the player has no choice. The agent only changes it if you ask.
  • For the player: with the Menu system, Options, Graphics, Ray tracing. The line only appears if their card has it and the game allows it; each click moves from Automatic to On then to Off. Their choice is kept from one play to the next.
  • For the editor's views: Preferences, Display section, View ray tracing (Automatic at first). It only applies to you; the views only use it if the game allows it. A camera's preview shows the game's, in Automatic.
  • Indirect lighting: for the player, the Indirect lighting line under Ray tracing in the menu (On at first, a click moves to Off, kept from one play to the next; a short line says what it does); for the editor's views, Preferences, View indirect lighting (on at first). It only works in High quality, with ray tracing on. A script reads and changes it with game.graphics and game.set_graphics (indirect_light).

When the graphics card's memory is full¶

Ray tracing keeps a copy of the scene in the graphics card's memory. The game gives itself a share of that memory (most of it on a dedicated card, half on an integrated chip, which shares the PC's memory), and ray tracing never takes more than a quarter of it, nor the room of models and images. When it is full, ray tracing stops arranging anything new, gives back what is used least, then reaches less far; as a last resort it turns itself off: shadows come back from shadow maps at the next frame, without a stutter. The player's menu says so under the Ray tracing line ("Off: the graphics card's memory is full (classic shadows)"), and so do the editor's Preferences. It tries again as soon as the choice or the quality changes.

Large model images that have not been shown for a few seconds may also drop to a small resolution meanwhile: they stay visible, a little blurry, and become sharp again by themselves when they are seen again and there is room again.

What we measured¶

On the city of the large-worlds bench (4 × 4 km with its countryside), at 1920 × 1080, High quality, on 10 November 2026:

  • on an NVIDIA RTX 5090, the card's time to draw a view stays the same: 0.17 ms in the street (shadow maps or rays), 0.15 against 0.18 ms flying over (where rays give the whole city its shadow, shadow maps only the first 150 metres); in the full city (100 % step), 0.39 ms with rays against 0.50 ms with shadow maps, and the whole frame goes from 4.4 to 3.2 ms. The scene arranged for rays takes 200 to 240 MB of the card (compacting gives back 440); everything is arranged in a little over 2 seconds at start-up, with no hitch over 3 ms;
  • on the integrated graphics chip of an AMD processor (forced On), the same view costs 9 to 10 ms more per frame (21 to 23 ms instead of 11 to 14), and the scene takes 260 to 600 MB of the PC's memory: too heavy for a smooth game. That is why Automatic never turns it on there;
  • reflections (on 10 November, on an island by the sea with polished metal, a mirror and a pane of glass) cost 0.05 to 0.1 ms per frame on the RTX 5090; in a city with nothing smooth, nothing. On the integrated chip they almost doubled the frame time (53 → 95 ms): where ray tracing is forced and heavy, they stay off;
  • when objects move (doors, physics), the scene arranged for rays is updated instead of rebuilt: in the full city, its preparation goes from 6 ms to 0.14 ms per frame.
  • what arrives (a tile, a model) is arranged for the rays in 2 ms at most per frame (1.5 in Medium), at the card's measured pace: on the integrated chip forced On, crossing the city went from 376 to 53 frames over 16.7 ms.
